# Nothing Happens After Eleven — Adventure Design

Approved in conversation on 2026-09-21. Night 3 of *No Further Action*, the campaign's first English-titled child. Design intent only; nothing here is canon or session history until the night is played and recapped.

## Contract

Late spring 1986, months after the Loman death file closed. This night is a **separate job**: no Abigail, no lake, no tower, no radio thread. It is the arc's **action** night — shots and live danger — and its discovery trail reaches Chief Whitley's local graft.

**Premise:** A salvage crew that has been quietly stripping the frozen Cinder Creek mill yard for months is loading out on a night the yard is supposed to be unwatched, when the watchman walks in by accident and the one patrolman who actually works the beat is close enough to hear it. Lilly must stop the load, keep people alive, and write a scene that can be written — and by dawn she understands that her chief has been keeping that lot off the record.

**Player-facing mission:** Answer a West End call that turns into a shooting, take the crew, and account for Len Pell.

**Resolution boundary (the arc split).** Night 3 ends with Lilly **certain** Whitley is corrupt and **unable to prove it**. Night 4 (`ashgrove-puzzle-1986`) is where she can build evidence and where the unfair walk lands. This supersedes the current wording in `CAMPAIGN.md`, `INDEX.md`, `world/npcs/chief-whitley.md`, and `world/lore/revelation-ladder.md`, which say night 3 is the likely night he comes off the force.

**The Concordance does nothing here.** No synchronization gap, no altered log a player can find, no nausea, no unnatural texture. Declare `supernatural_level: none` in the child front matter. The tell in this night means Whitley and only Whitley; a machine touching paper on the same night would poison it.

## Predetermined truths

- Cinder Creek Lumber's yard has been stripped in stages for **months**, not raided once.
- The crew works nights when the watchman is reliably absent: **Len Pell drinks at The Hoot Owl on Wednesdays and Saturdays**, which the whole town knows.
- On those nights Chief Whitley runs a quiet arrangement for cash: **Earl Voss is posted to patrol West End and the mill** (he will nest the cruiser and be asleep from roughly midnight to 5 a.m.), **Kevin Alder is posted away**, and if Lilly is still working she is given small business at the lake or in town. He steers assignments; he does not warn the crew directly.
- Whitley is paid **cash at a remove**, through a local go-between. No scrap hauler ever hands the chief money.
- Whitley knows only *salvage — keep the lot unpublished*. He does **not** know about the blasting magazine.
- Whitley is **not** Concordance and knows nothing of the consortium. This is ordinary small-town graft.
- **Tonight is one of those nights** — a Wednesday or a Saturday. The arrangement is running.
- **Tonight The Hoot Owl is closed** for a small private booking (a mill old-shift send-off supper; walk-ins turned away). The Owl crowd floods **Daisy's** next door for food.
- **Len does not sit in Daisy's** while his pub holds a private party. He goes to the yard instead. The crew keyed off the day of the week and never checked the Owl door.
- The crew is mixed: one **local ex-millwright**, laid off around 1983, who knows which buildings still lock, where the copper runs, and that the **powder magazine** past the stacks was never cleared when the company stopped building road; plus **two or three Rookton scrap haulers** with the flatbed and the buyer. One hauler runs the night and pays for the quiet.
- The Rookton men are plain scrap thieves. They are **not** the Rookton-side title holder in the Rennick split-title quarrel. The Rennicks stay off-screen.
- The magazine holds old, badly stored stock, sweating in a leftover bunker set away from the buildings. It is a hazard and a clock, not a required explosion.
- The crew kills the yard's remaining light and pulls power for their own gear, which **drops the West End houses**. A tenant phones the precinct around **00:20**.
- **Len walked into the loading crew.** They are thieves, not killers; the local panicked. Len's condition runs on a clock rather than a pre-decided outcome.
- **Kevin has been counting heavy flatbeds on 328 since winter** and never wrote it down, because a truck on a state highway is not a crime.
- The morning after, Whitley does **not** try to shrink a shooting with a cop on scene. He backs Lilly loudly, closes the salvage case, praises the work, and quietly ends the arrangement.

## The tell

Whitley's habit: he folds a sheet so the **heading disappears**, then creases it once with the side of his hand. He is physically making the problem smaller. Anyone can fold paper; this is a specific fold.

- **Opening drop.** At the start of the evening shift he does it to **last night's mill-noise complaint** while telling Lilly it is kids, or Earl's, and not detective work — and hands her Main Street instead.
- **Callback.** After the yard, that same complaint (or its carbon) surfaces in a night drawer, on a spike in the mill office, or in the stack that never needed a detective. Heading tucked, one crease.
- **What it is:** a habit she watched hours earlier, now on paper about the lot she was steered away from. A lawyer would call it a fold. She will not.

## Opening (why she is in earshot)

Whitley saw the same dark pub everyone else did. He assigns Lilly the **Daisy's overflow** on Main — compliance, keep it civil — which is real-looking, small, and nowhere near West End.

She does not love it. She stays at her desk doing paperwork, intending to drift down later and probably help Daisy close around one or two. So at **00:20** the West End call lands in an almost empty precinct with the detective sitting in it. The chief is at home. Nobody is available to make the call small in real time.

Her own small act of avoidance is what puts her in earshot. This is the explosive opening: the radio, not the briefing.

**Open procedural question for the write-up:** who takes a phone call at the Ashgrove precinct after midnight in 1986 is not established in any campaign file. Decide it during authoring (a desk/radio-room body, a roll to Rookton county dispatch and back, or a line ringing into a near-empty building with Lilly in it) rather than leaving a category where a value belongs.

## People

- **Chief Whitley** — smart, protective of the arrangement, never careless enough to put his best detective on the mill. He steers rather than forbids. He does not lecture, confess, or act cartoonish, and he is capable of praising her the next morning.
- **Kevin Alder** — this night's support and its second pair of hands. He takes the West End call because Earl does not answer, and he takes it harder than a lights-out call deserves because of the trucks he has been counting. A cop who sees a pattern and has nothing to file: a small rhyme for Lilly's whole night.
- **Earl Voss** — a hole in the beat, not a conspirator. Posted to West End, nested somewhere comfortable, asleep from about midnight. He is innocent, useless, and the reason the arrangement works.
- **Len Pell** — the question of the night. Territorial, on a Rennick key, in the wrong place because his pub was shut. Shut in somewhere, hurt, or past helping, depending on how fast Lilly gets there and how she searches. Daisy's brother-in-law, so the news travels through people.
- **The local ex-millwright** — competent, aggrieved, not a killer; the one who panics when the watchman appears. Afterward he can give Lilly the shape of it — *we were told the lot was safe Wednesdays and Saturdays* — without a name and without signing.
- **Rookton haulers** — here because a dead town is free inventory. They shoot to break contact and move the truck, not to hold ground.
- **Daisy Pell** — jammed with the Owl crowd, and Len's sister-in-law. She notices he is not where a drinking night puts him.
- **Sgt. Doyle** — home with his family when the call lands. Reachable at the cost of time; the mill call is too urgent to wait on him. Not the graft vector.

## Shape and mix

One session, solo, Novice Lilly. Same band as nights 1–2 (`format: one-shot`, `expected_runtime_hours: "3-5"`, `players: "1"`, `pc_mode: player-supplied`, `setting_modules: []`, house rules HR-NFA-001 and HR-NFA-002, cinematic accuracy). More action-weighted than night 2.

Beats are a pacing map, not a scene order.

1. **Explosive opening.** The tuck and the steer, then the 00:20 call into a nearly empty precinct.
2. **Choices.** The assigned Main Street job against the West End call; how fast to roll; whether to take Kevin, wake Doyle, or go alone; the fence line, the nested cruiser, the lit buildings, the truck.
3. **Reversal.** This is not a power cut and not kids. It is an organized load-out on ground the chief made small — and there is powder on the flatbed.
4. **Set piece.** The yard under the stacks: crew, truck, darkness, wet ground, the rail spur, the magazine as hazard and clock, Len somewhere in it. Objectives beyond winning the firefight: stop the load, find Len alive, keep the scene writable.
5. **Closure.** The crew is taken, scattered, or gone with part of the load. Lilly finds the fold. She can close a real case and still have nothing on her chief.

## Reachability

**Onto the ground.** Kevin's radio is the default. If she sits on it, Kevin goes in alone and the night worsens — a second call, shots carrying across the creek to Main, Len's clock advancing. If she took the Main Street assignment instead, Daisy raises Len's absence on a drinking night. Earl's nest is findable by anyone hunting Earl for any reason.

**To the conclusion that Whitley is dirty — four independent vectors, none of them evidence:**

1. **The fold** on last night's mill-noise complaint, hours after she watched him make it.
2. **The roster:** Earl on West End exactly on Len's drinking nights, Kevin posted away, her own nights aimed at the lake or Main. Readable in old assignment sheets, and entirely defensible as a chief assigning patrol.
3. **The local's unsigned line** about the lot being safe on Wednesdays and Saturdays. No name, never repeated.
4. **Kevin's counted trucks:** months of heavy flatbeds that never became a report.

**Forbidden this night:** a ledger, a signed statement, cash in hand, a confession, or a chain-of-command packet that names him. Tonight's shooting file is ordinary police paper, not proof of payoffs.

## Failure and endings

- Rolling slowly costs Len, costs Kevin, or lets the flatbed reach 328 with powder on it — it does not close the case.
- Losing the crew entirely still leaves the yard, the magazine, the tenant's call, the roster, and the fold.
- Full success: Len alive, the load stopped, the crew in the two cells, the powder secured.
- Costly success: the case closes but someone is dead, or the powder leaves the county.
- Failure: the load goes, the official story stays small, and Whitley's arrangement survives intact into night 4.

In every ending Lilly keeps the suspicion and lacks the proof. The hook is her certainty, not a name.

## Lethality and re-entry

The precinct is safe. The yard can kill once she has chosen to walk in. Because the table is solo, prepared re-entry matters: Kevin beside her, Doyle reachable by radio, and Len as a pair of hands if he is found in shape to use them.
