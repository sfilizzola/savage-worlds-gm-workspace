## Ashgrove as a town

### The Rennick sale (1989–90)

Margaret Rennick dies in 1989 (~75). Warren pushes to close the mill ground. The complication: the split title with a Rookton-side holding means Warren cannot sign alone. After Margaret is gone, the Rookton buyer likely applies legal pressure and Carol — who publicly held her mother's side but was privately worn down — lets go. Warren sells, pockets his share, and leaves Ashgrove for good. The mill era is formally over.

Don Halvorsen (Carol's husband, day patrol) lives with the family's aftermath at home. That thread is available to a future night if it needs a civic or property angle. No Rennick calls the detective.

### Outdoor economy (1990–95)

The valley geography supports hiking, fishing, kayaking and rafting on the Ashgrove River and Lake Barrow, mountain biking on Elk Ridge and Pine Mountain, summer camps, trail running, and road cycling on 328. Winter adds cross-country and snowshoe trails on the ridges — not a downhill resort (Elk Ridge tops at 3,240 ft; too low for reliable downhill snow, but fine for groomed Nordic tracks).

By 1995 a cluster of small adventure outfitters, a lakeside camp, a trail-running event circuit, and seasonal workers have reshaped the local economy. New people arrived; some stayed. Young entrepreneurs and outdoor-industry workers make up most of the new faces.

### Town growth

Ashgrove goes from ~1,000 people in 1986 to roughly ~2,000 by 1995–98. Still a town, not a city — but the faces are no longer all known. Plates from Portland and Eugene appear in the Daisy's parking lot on summer weekends.

### Mill plaza and West End

The Cinder Creek mill ground is redeveloped into a small commercial plaza: a few shops, a bar, a coffee roaster, a gear outfitter. The Cinder Creek yard that was dark in 1986 is now the new end of Main Street.

West End — once the mill-camp edge of town — becomes the interesting place to live. Close to the trailhead, close to the new plaza, cheaper than the old Main Street blocks when the first buyers moved in. Young people and entrepreneurs settled there. Gentrification is the word nobody in Ashgrove uses yet, but the Cedar Street rental crowd uses it in Portland.

### Precinct strain

The precinct was sized for 900 people. At 2,000 it starts to feel small — shift coverage, desk space, the evidence room. Hiring catches up slowly. By 1998 it is beginning to look like the wrong building for the town Ashgrove became.

## People Lilly knows

### Tom Doyle → Chief

Sarah Doyle leaves for college around 1989; Ben around 1993. Shortly after the kids are gone, Doyle steps into the chief's chair — the campaign establishes a shared-chair interim after Whitley walks, with county covering part of the load. Doyle taking it around 1993–96 closes that gap cleanly.

By 1998 he is Lilly's chief, not her floor sergeant. He signs off on her work. He manages Rook County politics she used to ignore. The relationship is still functional and still a little wary — he has always respected her work and never quite trusted her instincts.

### Whitley — still around, losing ground

He resigned or retired under the unfair walk. By 1998 he has been a civilian for years. He shows up at civic events, ribbon-cuttings, the Grange picnics — still presenting himself as the man the mayor should call. The town's growth brought people who do not know who he was. Politicians stopped returning his calls before he stopped placing them.

He holds the grudge against Lilly because she is the reason the heat ever landed, and because she is still there, still competent, still trusted by the man sitting in his chair. At least one 1998 night will require her to ask him something he resents giving.

### Abigail Carr — Lilly's closest friend

From early 1987, after the cases, they kept talking. Abigail is one year younger than Lilly (born 1963; 35 in 1998). Her voice on KCRK is the sound of the town's new texture — trail events, new businesses, summer crowd news, valley school sports. She knows everyone, hears everything, and tells Lilly what a radio host hears differently than a detective does.

Their friendship is not professional. It is the only relationship Lilly built in Ashgrove that started after she arrived and was never about a case. Abigail knows Lilly applied for the FBI consultancy more than once. She does not say anything when it comes up; she just refills the coffee.

## Lilly's life

### Housing

The Cedar Street municipal rental was always one year only. She finds somewhere else in 1987 — probably a rental on the quieter end of Spruce or Larch, somewhere a detective with no roots lands. By the mid-1990s, with the town growing and property moving, she buys a house. West End is the natural candidate: affordable when she looked, now the interesting part of town.

She did not intend to stay in Ashgrove this long. The house is the admission she has not made out loud.

### Career texture

Competent, steady, unremarkable in the best sense. Missing persons, robberies, a shooting in the vicinity, the occasional out-of-towner making a mess. Nothing like 1986 — no cases that pull at something underneath. From 1995 onward, the tourism growth brings new problems: underage drinking at the summer camps, noise complaints, a fight at the mill plaza bar, a seasonal worker who doesn't leave when the season ends.

Throughout the gap she applies for FBI consultancy work — several times, through the right channels. Nothing ever reaches Oregon. Nothing ever reaches Ashgrove. She assumes the Keane bargain left a longer shadow than she was told. She keeps applying anyway, less often by the mid-1990s.

The first FBI consultancy of her career is the day `federal-in-ashgrove` opens.

### The quiet moment — 1996

Her biological father dies in prison. She receives word through a letter or a call — either Captain Keane (who made the original bargain in 1986) or the old PI she hired as a teenager. Not dramatic. She reads it, sets it down, goes back to work.

The file she has always kept at the back of her mind closes one page and opens another. This is a private moment, not a case. She does not tell Abigail everything.

This feeds `sealed-paper` (1998 personal bruise). The death itself is the quiet gap event; what surfaces in 1998 is what the death released — the PI resurfacing with files, or Medrick revealing what he knew, or something that was waiting for the father to be gone before it could move.
